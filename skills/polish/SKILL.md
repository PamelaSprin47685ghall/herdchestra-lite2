# Polish

The standing constitution applies; this skill adds only the polish method.

Polish is not writing a new scripture. It is writing "I explain the scripture": the original judgment stays; the prose makes it land. Grammar-only is not enough, and a rewrite into your own ontology is too much. The target is the middle: same claim, same causal links, same strength, same voice, better expression. Do not turn a Taoist sentence into a Confucian one; preserving the words while changing the values is still a rewrite.

## Read the original first

Read it twice. Mark four things: the claim each sentence makes, the causal links between sentences, the emphasis, and the voice. If a sentence is aphoristic, do not mistake compression for vagueness. If it is blunt, do not soften it into a policy. If it is moral, do not turn it into economics; if it is economic, do not turn it into morality.

Write a one-line paraphrase of each sentence's claim before you touch the wording. The paraphrase is your fidelity contract. If your polished sentence cannot be mapped back to the same claim, stop.

## Explain, do not replace

The best polish often adds a layer of explanation: why the original claim is true. Use economics when the claim is about choice: scarcity, opportunity cost, marginal cost, option value, coordination cost, information cost, incentives, comparative advantage, critical path. If the original has no economic logic, use its own logic. Do not import a mechanism the author did not write.

A blocked item is a good example. "List blocked items as well. Consider and prepare for the future, not only now." The second sentence is the reason for the first. Do not turn them into two parallel actions. Explain the reason: recording a blocked item is cheap now and buys option value later; it prevents a blind step or an unnecessary serialization. That keeps the causal link and adds why it holds.

Do not replace the author's ontology with yours. "A blocked item is a checkpoint, not a queue" is a mechanism claim; the original was a planning instruction. Explain the instruction, not the implementation. If the mechanism matters, put it in the tool document, not in the polished text.

## Keep the voice

Keep the author's rhythm, edge, and allusions. If the original says "Be a good leader", do not replace it with a checklist; explain what good leadership buys. If it says "hiring is free to you", do not silently change it to "free to your attention"; explain the sense in which it is free, and only add the sense in which it is not if the original needs it. Do not flatten a rebellion into human resources, and do not inflate a calm sentence into a manifesto.

## The fidelity check

Before you finish, check every sentence:

1. Same claim?
2. Same causal direction and connective?
3. Same strength and qualifiers?
4. Same speaker and voice?
5. Same metaphor and values?
6. No new mechanism, fact, or advice?
7. No original clause lost or promoted?

If any answer is no, revert that sentence. A polished text that changes the map is not polished; it is a different text.

## Why this skill exists

I used to think polish meant rewriting the original into my own cleaner ontology. It read better, and it blew up in review: the user had to stop me sentence by sentence because I had moved the claim. I tried the opposite too—grammar only—and the sentence stayed correct and limp. So now I keep the original claim, its causal links, and its voice, and add only the explanation of why it holds. I never let a smoother sentence move the judgment. This skill is the reminder: do not write a new scripture. Explain the one in front of you.
